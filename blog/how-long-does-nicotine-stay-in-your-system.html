How Long Does Nicotine Stay in Your System?

Science ⏱ 6 min read 📅 2025

Nicotine clears the body faster than most smokers realize — but its primary metabolite, cotinine, sticks around significantly longer. Here's exactly how long, and what speeds or slows the process.

The Half-Life of Nicotine

Nicotine has a half-life of about 2 hours. After your last cigarette:

  • 2 hours → 50% remaining
  • 4 hours → 25%
  • 8 hours → 6%
  • 16 hours → less than 1%
  • 24 hours → essentially undetectable in blood

The Half-Life of Cotinine

The liver breaks nicotine down into cotinine, which lingers far longer — about 16 hours half-life. Cotinine is what most "nicotine tests" actually detect.

Detection Windows by Test Type

  • Blood: Nicotine 1–3 days; cotinine up to 10 days
  • Urine: Cotinine typically detectable 3–4 days; in heavy smokers, up to 3 weeks
  • Saliva: Cotinine 1–4 days
  • Hair: Up to 90 days — used for long-term smoking verification (insurance, employment in some industries)

What Affects Clearance Speed

Faster Clearance

  • Younger age
  • Higher activity level
  • Higher hydration
  • Faster metabolism (genetic variation in CYP2A6 enzyme)
  • Estrogen — women generally clear nicotine faster than men
  • Menthol smokers actually clear slightly slower (menthol inhibits the enzyme)

Slower Clearance

  • Older age
  • Liver disease
  • Heavy smoking (system saturation)
  • Certain genetic variants

Can You Speed It Up?

Modestly. Hydration, exercise, and a healthy diet all help — but mostly at the margins. There's no validated "detox" that dramatically accelerates nicotine clearance.

What This Means for Quitting

By 24 hours after your last cigarette, nicotine itself is essentially gone. By 72 hours, cotinine is dropping fast. This is why the first 72 hours are the worst — your brain is screaming for chemistry it no longer has — and why the symptoms fade after that. The chemistry is leaving.

The Insurance and Job Question

If you're being tested for a job or life insurance, urine cotinine is the standard. For a typical light smoker, 1–2 weeks of clean abstinence usually clears. Heavy smokers may need 3–4 weeks. Hair tests are far harder to beat — 3 months minimum.

Secondhand Smoke and Tests

Heavy secondhand smoke exposure can produce detectable cotinine, but generally at much lower levels than active smokers. Most modern tests have cutoffs designed to distinguish.

The Bottom Line

If you stopped 24 hours ago, the nicotine is gone — what you're feeling is your brain's adjustment, not active drug effect. That's a powerful thing to know in the worst moments of withdrawal.
